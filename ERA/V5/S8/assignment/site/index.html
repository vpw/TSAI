Twenty-one mechanisms, arranged by the date they actually appeared rather than by family or by teaching order. Ordered this way the field stops looking like a catalogue of tricks and starts looking like an argument — one where you can see what people were worried about, and when they stopped worrying about it.

act 4 · the bonus question

What the timeline shows that a list would not

The short answer

A list of attention mechanisms answers what exists. Put the same mechanisms on a real time axis and they answer something better: which cost was hurting, and when. The lanes make that legible — early work attacks compute, the recent wave attacks memory and length — but the ordering alone is not the finding.

The finding is in the gaps. Again and again the idea arrives years before the pressure that makes anyone adopt it. MQA solves the KV-cache problem in 2019 and is ignored until serving costs bite in 2023. The delta rule is published before RoPE and then waits three years for an algorithm that suits a GPU. Nothing was missing technically in either case; what was missing was a reason to care. A list flattens those waits to zero and turns a story about economic pressure into a story about invention.

And the density says the rest. Nothing for two years after the Transformer, then a genuine 21-month silence between ALiBi and GQA with nothing in it at all, then five mechanisms inside four months in 2023 — two of them eight days apart, neither with a paper. Even the field's busiest efficiency stretch, in late 2024, carries one exception: the bonus mechanism, paying more compute for less noise while everything around it pays less compute for less quality. Ordering tells you the sequence; spacing tells you the pressure.

act 5 · sources

Every date, and how it was checked

The hard part of this assignment was never the code — it was the dates. What follows is the working, row by row: the primary source, the exact record the date came from, and the caveats. Where a first draft was wrong, the row says so rather than quietly showing the corrected value.

tier A Read straight off the source's own submission record. Nothing to dispute.
tier B The link from mechanism to paper came out of a different paper's bibliography, then the paper was re-checked against arXiv directly.
tier C No paper exists; the date comes from an archive. The weakest class here — and still pinned to a specific minute.

Check it yourself

Every arXiv date above is the v1 submission timestamp, never the latest revision — several of these papers are on v5 or v7, and using the revision date would silently reorder the timeline. One request returns all nineteen records; published is v1, updated is the newest revision.
