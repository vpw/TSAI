Analysis: how this tokenizer was arrived at, and why it works

This tab is the reasoning behind the numbers on the Overview tab — the design decisions, the dead ends, and why the final configuration actually satisfies the assignment's scoring objective rather than just happening to. For the exact settings/recipe, see the Methodology tab.

1. The real constraint: a shared 10,000-token budget across four very unequal corpora

English and Hindi's India articles are several times longer than Telugu's and Marathi's (word counts: ). Training one BPE model on the raw, unweighted concatenation of all four spends most of its merge budget on whichever language supplies the most raw text — which is not the same thing as whichever language needs the most merges to hit a good ratio. Every subsequent decision follows from managing that budget deliberately instead of letting corpus size decide it by default.

2. Why byte-level and character-level BPE were structurally the wrong tool here

Byte-level BPE (GPT-2/RoBERTa-style) pre-tokenizes into raw UTF-8 bytes. Devanagari and Telugu characters are 3 bytes each, so under a shared 10,000-token budget, a large share of merges gets consumed just reassembling individual Indic characters from their byte triplets before any real subword structure can be learned. That shows up directly in the numbers: byte-level couldn't even reach the full 10,000-token budget ( tokens learned) and produced the worst ratios across the board (spread , score ).

Character-level BPE avoids the byte-fallback problem (its base alphabet is already whole Unicode characters), and with no pre-tokenizer boundary it does reach the full 10,000-token vocab. But merges are then free to span whitespace and word boundaries however the raw corpus mixture's character-pair frequencies happen to fall — so, like byte-level, the language whose text dominates the input has the strongest pull on which merges get learned, independent of the actual per-language weighting knob. It landed second-worst (spread , score ).

Word-level BPE restricts merges to within a single pre-tokenized word (Sennrich-style). This is the key structural property that makes the per-language oversampling weights actually work as a control knob: because a word from language L can only ever contribute merges built from L's own characters, repeating language L's text more or less directly and predictably shifts how much of the 10,000-token budget L receives. That controllability is what made the rest of the tuning process possible at all.

3. First attempt: reweighting alone plateaued

The first pass used a single symmetric oversampling exponent (XLM-R-style, share ∝ size^α) applied uniformly to Hindi/Telugu/Marathi. It pulled the three Indic languages closer together, but left English's ratio (X1) around 1.33 — comfortably above the assignment's ≤~1.2 target — because reweighting only changes which language's text supplies more training data; it doesn't change the trainer's own criterion for which pairs are "frequent enough to merge." Increasing Telugu's weight far beyond that point (up to 12–28×) kept moving Telugu's own ratio but stopped moving English's at all — a plateau around X1 ≈ 1.27–1.31, confirmed by a full grid search.

4. The lever that actually closed the gap: min_frequency

The Hugging Face BPE trainer's default min_frequency=2 means a candidate merge is only learned if the pair co-occurs at least twice in the (weighted) training data. With a 10,000-token budget shared across four languages, English's own distinctive multi-syllable words (word counts run into the thousands, but many technical/proper-noun words appear only once) kept losing out to shared, cross-lingual, or more frequent pieces — the vocabulary kept re-using generically useful merges instead of ever getting around to English-specific ones. Lowering min_frequency to 1 let the trainer learn merges that occur even a single time, which is exactly the profile of English's long tail of once-occurring words. That one change dropped X1 from ≈1.27 to ≈1.21 at the same corpus weighting — more effective than any amount of further reweighting had been.

5. Why the final configuration satisfies the (two-layer) objective

The assignment's scoring is not "minimize spread" in isolation — it explicitly anchors English's ratio at ≤~1.2 first, and only then rewards a small spread. The final search therefore optimized in that order: reject any candidate where X1 > 1.2, and among the survivors, minimize the gap to whichever of Hindi/Telugu/Marathi is furthest from English. The winning configuration () lands at for English — just inside the target — while keeping the worst-case outlier (Telugu) within of it, giving a spread of and a score of .

It's a confirmed local optimum, not a global guarantee: a fine-grained search immediately around this point found nothing better, but a fundamentally different strategy (e.g. allocating separate fixed sub-vocabularies per language instead of one shared, weighted model) was not exhaustively tried and might do better still.

6. Why SentencePiece — the XLM-R family algorithm — didn't win here

SentencePiece BPE was trained on the identical weighted mixture and evaluated the same way, and it's a strong second place (score ). But SentencePiece's trainer has no equivalent of min_frequency to tune, so it plateaus at the same kind of ceiling the word-level tokenizer hit in step 3 — X1 ≈ 1.31, above target — with no remaining lever to pull. This is a case where the algorithm most associated with state-of-the-art multilingual models (XLM-R) is not automatically the best fit for this specific, small-scale, hard-constrained scoring objective.

Methodology

1. Corpus

One Wikipedia article per language — the article about India itself — fetched via the Wikipedia API, with localized titles resolved through the English article's cross-language links: .

2. Scoring objective (corrected reading)

The assignment's own wording anchors English: "(Total English Vocab)/(Total English tokens) must be around 1.2 or less" — X1. Ratios for Hindi/Telugu/Marathi (X2–X4) are computed the same way. The four are sorted and scored as 1000/(X_max − X_min). This means the optimization target is two-layered, not a single symmetric "minimize spread": first satisfy X1 ≤ ~1.2, then minimize the gap between English and whichever of the other three languages is the worst-case outlier.

3. Vocabulary strategy: one joint 10,000-token BPE model

Rather than allocating a fixed sub-budget of the 10,000 tokens per language, a single BPE model is trained on a mixture of all four corpora, so the vocabulary can allocate more merges to whichever language actually needs them. Smaller corpora (Telugu and Marathi are far shorter than the English/Hindi articles) are oversampled by repeating their text before training, so the trainer doesn't starve them of merge budget.

4. The min_frequency lever

Reweighting the corpus mixture alone plateaued with English's X1 around 1.27–1.31, no matter how aggressively Telugu/Marathi were oversampled. Lowering the BPE trainer's min_frequency from the default 2 to 1 let it learn rarer, English-specific merges instead of always reusing shared, cross-lingual pieces — closing most of the remaining gap to the ≤1.2 target on its own.

5. Tuning search

A grid/local search over per-language repeat counts (English fixed at the minimum, 1) was run twice — once at the HF default min_frequency=2 (plateaued above target), then again at min_frequency=1 — optimizing first for the X1 ≤ 1.2 constraint, then for minimizing the worst-case gap to English among Hindi/Telugu/Marathi. Byte-level and character-level BPE were dropped early as dominated on every axis and kept only as comparison baselines (see Overview tab).

6. X vs. fertility — two different, complementary numbers

The assignment's X is a unique-vocabulary ratio: unique words in the article divided by distinct token IDs needed to spell all of them. It is not the same as fertility (tokens produced per word across the full running text, including repeats), which for this same tokenizer runs from about 1.26 (Telugu) to 1.64 (Marathi) tokens per word. Both describe compression behavior, but X is what the assignment scores on.
